Chapter 2

  1. Oxygen gas: element. Sea water: mixture. Carbon dioxide: compound. Gold: element. Air: mixture.
  2. Oxygen-18: 8 protons, 18 − 8 = 10 neutrons, 8 electrons. 16O2−: 8 protons, 8 neutrons, 8 + 2 = 10 electrons.
  3. Both have 7 protons (and 7 electrons in the neutral atom), so both are nitrogen with the same chemistry. Nitrogen-14 has 7 neutrons and nitrogen-15 has 8, so they differ in mass.
  4. MgCl2 (one 2+ charge balanced by two 1− charges). Na3PO4 (three 1+ charges balance one 3− charge).
  5. CH4: 12.011 + 4 × 1.0080 = 16.043. NH3: 14.007 + 3 × 1.0080 = 17.031.

Chapter 3

  1. 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p4; six valence electrons. Oxygen is in the same group.
  2. Removing one electron leaves Na+ with the arrangement of neon, a filled shell. Removing a second electron would break into this filled shell, which, like the shell of neon itself (first ionisation energy 21.56 eV), holds its electrons very tightly. The second electron therefore costs far more energy than the first (5.14 eV).
  3. H–S–H, with two lone pairs on sulfur, which has six valence electrons like oxygen. Four electron pairs around sulfur give a bent molecule, like water.
  4. Formaldehyde: carbon in the centre, single bonds to two hydrogen atoms, a double bond to oxygen, and two lone pairs on oxygen; C–O bond order 2. Hydrogen cyanide: H–C\(\equiv\)N with one lone pair on nitrogen; C–N bond order 3.
  5. Carbon forms 4 bonds (to three H and to O), oxygen 2 (to C and to H), each hydrogen 1. The structure is H3C–O–H: a carbon bonded to three hydrogen atoms and to an oxygen, which carries the fourth hydrogen and two lone pairs.

Chapter 4

  1. CH3CH2CH3. The skeletal formula is a single bent line with two segments (a “V”): each end is a CH3 group and the corner is a CH2 group.
  2. The carbon at the line end (the CH3 group) carries three hydrogen atoms; the carbon bonded to both oxygen atoms carries none, since it already has four bonds (one to carbon, two to the double-bonded oxygen, one to OH). Adding the hydrogen of the OH group gives C2H4O2.
  3. A chain of three carbon atoms with an OH group on the middle carbon: H3C–CH(OH)–CH3, with every hydrogen drawn. Its molecular formula is C3H8O, the same as propan-1-ol, but the OH group is on a different carbon, so the two compounds are isomers. (This compound is propan-2-ol.)
  4. In cyclohexane, each carbon is a corner with two single bonds and carries two hydrogen atoms: C6H12. In benzene, each carbon has one double and one single bond in the ring and carries one hydrogen atom: C6H6.
  5. Above the ring: the CH2OH group on carbon 4 and the OH group on carbon 1. Below the ring: the OH groups on carbons 2 and 3. The remaining positions carry hydrogen atoms (below on carbons 1 and 4, above on carbons 2 and 3).

Chapter 5

  1. Differences in electronegativity: C–H 0.35, N–H 0.84, C–O 0.89, O–H 1.24. From least to most polar: C–H < N–H < C–O < O–H. The \(\delta\)− end is C in C–H, N in N–H, and O in C–O and O–H.
  2. Carbon dioxide is linear, so its two C=O bond dipoles point in exactly opposite directions and cancel. Sulfur dioxide is bent, so its two S–O bond dipoles do not cancel, and the molecule is polar.
  3. Each of the three N–H groups is a hydrogen-bond donor; the lone pair on nitrogen is an acceptor. Yes: an N–H group of one molecule can point at the lone pair of another, forming N–H···N.
  4. Ethanol has an O–H group that can donate and accept hydrogen bonds, so it fits into the hydrogen-bond network of water. Hexane has only C–C and C–H bonds; it is non-polar and cannot form hydrogen bonds, so it is excluded from water by the hydrophobic effect.
  5. Ice would sink as it formed. Lakes would freeze from the bottom upwards, and in a cold climate they could freeze solid, instead of being protected by a floating layer of ice with liquid water beneath.

Chapter 7

  1. C2H5OH + 3 O2 → 2 CO2 + 3 H2O. (Check: 2 C, 6 H and 7 O on each side.)
  2. 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O, so one mole of water forms per mole of H2. 4.0 g of H2 (molar mass 2.016 g mol−1) is 1.98 mol, giving 1.98 mol × 18.015 g mol−1 = 35.7 g, about 36 g of water.
  3. HCl donates a proton and is the acid; NH3 accepts it and is the base.
  4. CO: oxygen −2, so carbon +2. NH3: hydrogen +1 each, so nitrogen −3. N2: an element, so 0.
  5. Water is released. Glycine is C2H5NO2 and alanine C3H7NO2; together C5H12N2O4, minus H2O, gives C5H10N2O3.

Chapter 8

  1. K = [NH3]2 / ([N2] [H2]3).
  2. [AB]/[A] = K[B]. At [B] = 0.1 M: 100 × 0.1 = 10, so AB : A = 10 : 1 and 10/11 \(\approx\) 91 % of A is joined. At [B] = 10 \(\mu\)M = 10−5 M: 100 × 10−5 = 10−3, so only about 0.1 % of A is joined.
  3. pH = −log10(2.0 × 10−5) = 4.70. It is acidic (below 7).
  4. log10([HCOO−]/[HCOOH]) = 5.75 − 3.75 = 2, so the ratio is 102 = 100.
  5. In the buffer, the added H3O+ ions are taken up by HPO42−, forming H2PO4−; the ratio of the two forms changes only slightly, so the pH barely moves. Pure water has no such base to take up the protons, so all the added H3O+ remains free and the pH falls sharply.

Chapter 9

  1. ΔH° = ΔfH°(H2O, l) − ΔfH°(H2O, g) = −285.830 − (−241.826) = −44.004 kJ mol−1. Condensation releases heat: it is exothermic, the reverse of evaporation.
  2. ΔG = 20 − 298.15 × 0.100 = −9.8 kJ mol−1, so yes, it is spontaneous at 25 °C. ΔG = 0 at T = ΔH/ΔS = 20 000/100 = 200 K; the reaction is spontaneous at all temperatures above 200 K.
  3. −11.4 kJ mol−1 is about two steps of 5.71 kJ mol−1 in the favourable direction, so K \(\approx\) 102 = 100.
  4. Q > K, so ΔG = RT ln(Q/K) is positive and the reaction proceeds backwards, converting products into reactants until Q falls to K.
  5. Joining two molecules into one reduces the number of independent particles, so ΔS is negative; if the joining releases heat, ΔH is also negative. This is the third case of Table 9.1: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, and the term −TΔS is positive and grows with T. The joining is therefore most favourable at low temperature and becomes less favourable as the temperature rises; equivalently, K of an exothermic reaction decreases on heating.
