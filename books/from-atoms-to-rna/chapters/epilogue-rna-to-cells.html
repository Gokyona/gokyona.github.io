Epilogue: From RNA to Cells

This book has followed one chemical path: from the formation of the elements, through small molecules and nucleotides, to chains of RNA that fold, catalyse and, under laboratory conditions, copy other RNA. A self-copying molecule is not yet a living cell. This epilogue outlines the next steps and states where the frontier of knowledge lies.

Compartments

A replicating molecule in open solution shares its products with every other molecule nearby. A ribozyme that improves replication helps its competitors as much as itself, and selection cannot favour it. Selection for function requires that the products of a sequence stay with that sequence. A compartment, a closed boundary that holds a set of molecules together, provides this link. A compartment that contains a replicating genetic system is called a protocell.

Models of protocell membranes are made from fatty acids: molecules with a carboxylic acid head group (Functional groups) and a long hydrocarbon tail. Such molecules are amphiphilic, having a polar part that interacts favourably with water and a non-polar part that does not. In water, above a critical concentration, they assemble into micelles, small aggregates with the tails inside and the head groups facing the water, or into vesicles: closed membranes, made of a double layer of molecules, that enclose a volume of water. The hydrophobic effect (Forces between molecules) drives this assembly. Fatty acid membranes are simpler than the phospholipid membranes of modern cells, and are more permeable to small molecules.

Laboratory work has shown that such vesicles can carry out several functions of a cell. Hanczyc, Fujikawa and Szostak found that the clay montmorillonite, known to catalyse the polymerisation of RNA (Help from surfaces: montmorillonite), “accelerates the spontaneous conversion of fatty acid micelles into vesicles”; RNA adsorbed to clay can be encapsulated within vesicles; and “once formed, such vesicles can grow by incorporating fatty acid supplied as micelles and can divide without dilution of their contents by extrusion through small pores” (Hanczyc, Fujikawa, and Szostak 2003). Zhu and Szostak described a simpler pathway: large multilamellar vesicles fed with fatty acid micelles grow into long thread-like vesicles, and “modest shear forces are then sufficient to cause the thread-like vesicles to divide into multiple daughter vesicles without loss of internal contents”; encapsulated RNA is distributed to the daughters, and the protocells “can proceed through multiple cycles of reproduction” (Zhu and Szostak 2009).

Coupling genome and compartment

TipKey experiment: Chen, Roberts and Szostak 2004

Chen, Roberts and Szostak showed that “RNA, encapsulated in fatty acid vesicles, exerts an osmotic pressure on the vesicle membrane that drives the uptake of additional membrane components, leading to membrane growth at the expense of relaxed vesicles, which shrink.” The authors concluded: “Thus, more efficient RNA replication could cause faster cell growth, leading to the emergence of Darwinian evolution at the cellular level” (Chen, Roberts, and Szostak 2004). The coupling requires no function of the RNA other than its presence, and its replication.

Copying chemistry and membranes must also be compatible. Fatty acid membranes allow small charged molecules, such as nucleotides, to pass, and activated nucleotides added outside a model protocell can cross the membrane and take part in template copying inside it (Mansy et al. 2008). The Mg2+ ions needed for non-enzymatic copying disrupt fatty acid membranes, but Adamala and Szostak found that citrate protects the membranes from high Mg2+ concentrations while allowing RNA copying to proceed (Adamala and Szostak 2013).

From RNA to proteins

Modern cells make proteins on the ribosome, a large complex of RNA and protein. The structure of the large ribosomal subunit showed that the site at which peptide bonds are formed consists of RNA: the ribosome is a ribozyme (Nissen et al. 2000). As noted in Templating, Orgel regarded this as making it “almost certain that there was once an RNA World” (Orgel 2004). How an RNA world began to make proteins, how the genetic code arose, and how DNA took over the storage of genetic information are questions beyond the scope of this book.

The open frontier

Each chapter of this book has separated what is established from what is hypothesised. The established parts are substantial: the elements of RNA other than hydrogen are made in stars (Chapter 1); its building blocks form in plausible prebiotic reactions (Chapters 4 and 5); activated nucleotides join into chains on minerals and in ice (Chapter 6); random RNA sequences fold, and some of them bind and catalyse (Chapter 7); RNA enzymes made in the laboratory copy RNA and replicate exponentially (Chapter 8); and vesicles grow, divide and compete (this epilogue).

The open questions lie at the joins between these parts. Each step has been demonstrated under its own conditions, often with purified reagents and controlled cycles, and the conditions differ from step to step. No experiment has yet started from simple feedstock and arrived at a replicating, evolving system. The quantitative gaps are largest at copying: measured non-enzymatic copying is far too slow to sustain heredity from scratch (How fast is fast enough?), and laboratory ribozyme polymerases are far longer and more complex than the chains that prebiotic chemistry produces.

A further requirement became clear in the author’s simulations of protocells. Where heredity and vesicles coexisted, vesicle families carrying copied sequences won a single selective sweep, after which copying refilled every vesicle to the same load, and no heritable variation remained for selection to act on (Wahiro 2026). In that model, sustained evolution would need a sequence-dependent trait that copying does not erase, a sequence that does something, for example by catalysing its own copying, building its own membrane or protecting its compartment. This is the link between Chapters 7 and 8 that remains to be found: a sequence that is both copied and useful, in a chemistry and an environment where both happen at once.

The origin of life is therefore not a solved problem. It is a problem whose parts can now be stated precisely, measured in the laboratory and tested in models, which is the condition for solving it.
