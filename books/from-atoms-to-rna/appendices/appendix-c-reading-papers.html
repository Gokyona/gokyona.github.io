Appendix C — How to Read a Scientific Paper

Every key experiment in this book is taken from a published research paper, and every claim is linked to its source in the References. This appendix describes how such papers are organised and how to read them critically.

C.1 Kinds of publication

  • A research article reports new results: an experiment, an observation, a calculation or a model. It is the primary source for a claim.
  • A review summarises and evaluates the research articles on a topic. Reviews are the best entry point into an unfamiliar field, but a number quoted in a review should be checked in the research article it cites.
  • A preprint is a manuscript made public before, or without, formal peer review, on a server such as arXiv, bioRxiv or Zenodo.
  • Peer review is the evaluation of a manuscript by independent experts before publication in a journal. It reduces errors but does not guarantee that a result is correct; the test of a result is whether other laboratories can reproduce it.

Each publication is identified by a digital object identifier (DOI), a persistent string such as 10.1126/science.1060786. Prefixing it with https://doi.org/ gives a link that resolves to the publication. The DOIs of the works cited in this book are listed in the References.

C.2 The structure of a research article

Most research articles in chemistry and biology follow the same order:

  1. Title and abstract. The abstract states the question, the approach, the main result and its significance, usually in a single paragraph. It is the part of a paper most often quoted, and the key experiment boxes of this book quote abstracts directly wherever possible.
  2. Introduction. The background, the open problem and the aim of the work.
  3. Methods (or Materials and Methods, sometimes placed at the end or in supplementary material). The exact conditions: concentrations, temperatures, pH, times, instruments and analysis. Whether a result is relevant to the early Earth often depends on these details.
  4. Results. The observations, usually organised around figures and tables.
  5. Discussion. The interpretation of the results, their limitations and their relation to earlier work.
  6. References and supplementary information, which often contain the full data.

C.3 Reading critically

A paper is not read once from beginning to end. A practical order is: title and abstract, then the figures and their legends, then the results and methods for the parts that matter, and finally the discussion. The following questions help to evaluate a claim.

  • What exactly was measured? Distinguish the observation (for example, the products detected after a reaction) from the interpretation (for example, the mechanism proposed for their formation).
  • Under what conditions? Concentrations, temperature, pH, purity of reagents and duration determine whether a result could apply to a natural environment. Many experiments in Chapters 4 to 8 use concentrations or purified starting materials that are chosen to make a single step work.
  • How large is the effect, and how certain? Look for the numbers, their units and their uncertainties. A rate enhancement of 36 000 and one of 7 million (Section 7.6, Section 7.5) are both catalysis, but they differ by more than two orders of magnitude.
  • What controls were run? A control is an experiment identical to the main one except for the factor under test, such as a reaction without the template or without the catalyst. Without controls, an effect cannot be attributed to its proposed cause.
  • Has the result been reproduced? Results confirmed by independent groups are more secure than single reports.
  • Do the conclusions follow from the data? A statement in the discussion may go beyond the results; the abstract may state the most favourable interpretation.

C.4 Established, hypothesis, open question

The book distinguishes three kinds of statement, as described in the Preface. When reading a paper, the same distinction is useful:

  • A result is established when it rests on reproducible measurements under stated conditions.
  • An explanation of how the result fits into the origin of life, such as a proposed prebiotic setting, is usually a hypothesis.
  • What the paper does not address, or names as a limitation, often marks an open question.

For example, the experiment of Powner, Gerland, and Sutherland (2009) established a chemical route to activated pyrimidine nucleotides under stated laboratory conditions; whether the starting materials were available together on the early Earth is a hypothesis that the paper discusses but cannot settle by itself.

C.5 Numbers and models

Some results in this book are calculations rather than experiments: nearest-neighbour energies, melting curves, chain-length distributions and error thresholds. A calculation is only as good as its inputs. For each one, check which parameters were used, where they came from, under what conditions they were measured, and which effects the model omits. Calculations made for this book are marked as the author’s calculations, and their parameters and sources are given where they appear.

Powner, M. W., B. Gerland, and J. D. Sutherland. 2009. “Synthesis of Activated Pyrimidine Ribonucleotides in Prebiotically Plausible Conditions.” Nature 459 (7244): 239–42. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08013.
